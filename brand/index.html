Brand and voice guide

How VoiceClaw looks, sounds, and behaves.

One reference for every VoiceClaw surface: the website, the rack card, the business card, the trade-show banners, the LinkedIn page, the product, and any app that comes next. Every value here is lifted from what has already shipped, so following this guide keeps new work matching old work.

Version 2, written 2026-09-17 Sources voiceclaw.ca, the rack card, the business card, the trade-show backdrop and pull-up, the product one-liner Owner Ian Pilon

Essence

Before any colour or word: what VoiceClaw is, who it is for, and the one idea everything hangs on.

What it is

A managed business memory you can talk to. Ask about any asset, customer, or job and get an answer by voice or text instead of chasing people.

That sentence is final. Use it verbatim as the product description: the page title, LinkedIn About, decks, proposals, and anywhere a sentence describes the product.

The short form: A business memory you can talk to. Use it on headlines and print, where "managed" costs a line: the site H1, the rack card, the business card, the pull-up banner. It is always followed by the two knockout lines: "Ask by voice or text." "Get the answer."

Who it is for

Owner-operators who are tired of being the filing system. They own and run a business with a crew of five to fifty that services, rents, leases, or maintains physical things. Their records live in three or more places, and one of those places is a person.

The one idea

The answers were buried. We did the digging. The data was never easy to get. It was scattered across old software, texts, PDFs, and people's heads, and we clawed it out. Now every answer is a question away.

"Claw" is a deliberate brand plant. Use it as a verb ("clawed out of a legacy system") when the point is that we did hard work nobody else would.

Four positioning lines

A managed service, not software. We dig out your records, we build one memory, we run it. You just talk to it. Four beats: dig, build, run, talk.

A layer, not a replacement. Everything they already run stays.

Memory, not reception. It answers the owner. It does not answer the phone.

Then you just talk to it. The payoff line after the digging. It closes How it works on the site and the rack card.

Two colours, two jobs. Chartreuse says "this is VoiceClaw" and "this is the answer". Indigo says "press this". They never swap: no green button, no indigo headline word, no two-colour wave. If a page needs a third emphasis colour, the hierarchy is wrong, not the palette.

No light theme. VoiceClaw is dark only. Paint the ground explicitly on every surface so it holds on any host. A green glow behind a panel turns muddy yellow: glow the text and the dots, not the backgrounds.

The headline is plain. The site H1 and the print headlines sit in off-white with no highlighted word. The two knockout lines under them carry the colour. Section titles and the closing question keep one highlighted phrase.

The highlighted phrase. On a section title, one phrase turns chartreuse with a glow: the payoff word, not the subject. "answers?" "behaves." Never two phrases, never a whole line.

Weight rules. 500 for headings and buttons, 400 for text, 300 for big numbers. The knockout line is the one place weight 600 is allowed, because navy on chartreuse needs the mass. Nothing else is bold. Emphasis comes from colour and size, not weight.

Inter only. One face for everything, headlines included. Do not introduce a second face.

Phones. Under 760 px the H1 is min(38px,10vw) so it holds two lines on a 360 px screen. Balance the wrap on the note under the buttons.

Glow and depth

Glow is the signature on screen. It marks what is live, what is the answer, and what matters. Depth comes from surface lightness and soft shadows, not from lines. Print has no glow at all.

Three glow recipes cover everything: text-shadow for a phrase or numeral, box-shadow for a dot, and a ring at the soft glow value for a focused or hovered input. The button's hover glow is indigo, its own colour.

/* phrase or numeral */
color: var(--vc-accent);
text-shadow: 0 0 26px var(--vc-glow);
/* live dot, bullet, mic */
background: var(--vc-accent);
box-shadow: 0 0 10px 2px var(--vc-glow);
/* focus or hover ring */
border-color: var(--vc-accent);
box-shadow: 0 0 0 3px var(--vc-glow-soft);

Shadows are soft and large. The phone panel uses 0 32px 64px -24px rgba(0,0,0,.75) plus an indigo edge. A hovered primary button lifts one pixel and casts 0 10px 30px -10px of indigo glow. No hard edges, no 1 px borders on everything.

The hero lift. Behind a hero, a radial gradient of Lift at the top-left corner fading to nothing. It reads as a light source, not a decoration. Once per page.

The voice wave. Behind the phone on the site, two bundles of thin indigo lines on a flat baseline with speech-shaped bursts, at 40 percent opacity. One colour only. It is a still frame under reduced motion.

No glow in print. Chrome's PDF engine turns a large glow into a solid box, and spot inks cannot fade. Print pieces use flat colour and let the paper do the contrast.

Anatomy of an answer. A question in a slate bubble. A lookup chip with a chartreuse dot that names where it looked. Then the answer on a raised bubble: a small label naming the record, one big light number or state, and one sentence of detail with a name and a date in it.

The question is slate, not green, because the question is not the answer. The big value is the answer. The sentence is the proof. Together they let the owner check it without opening anything.

Layout

Mobile first, one column that grows to two. Calm density: room inside every block, muted secondary text, and almost no boxes.

Measure. Content maxes at 1120 px with a side gutter of clamp(20px,4vw,48px). Running text stays under about 62 characters.

Rhythm. Sections are separated by generous padding and, at most, one hairline. Inside a section, kicker, then title, then lead, then the content.

Radii. 5 px chips, knockout lines, and bubble tails, 16 px panels, 17 px bubbles, 42 px the phone, 999 px pills and inputs.

Not everything is a card. On the website the only bordered panel is the phone demo. Lists, steps, and fit columns sit directly on the ground and are separated by space.

Motion. Small and purposeful: a 1.4 s pulse on a live dot, a single border pulse when a suggestion lands, a 0.18 s pop on a new bubble, the slow voice wave. Every animation is off under reduced motion.

Verify at three widths. 1280, 390, and 360. No horizontal scroll at any of them. The 360 check is a real Samsung: the H1 must hold two lines and the hero buttons stack full width.

Print

Five print pieces exist. They are the site's hero and How it works, cut to size. Flat colour, spot inks, off-white as the paper itself.

Inks. Two spot inks, no process. PANTONE 2768 C is the navy, flooded to the bleed on both sides. The chartreuse is a fluorescent spot ink, never a CMYK build. The full colour rules are in Print colour, below.

Off-white is the paper. Print on bright white stock and knock the text out of the navy. No white ink, no third colour. The screen PDFs preview in brand RGB; print to the named inks, not the preview.

Draw, do not screenshot. Cards and flyers are drawn straight to vector PDF with reportlab (the draw_card.py and draw_rack.py pattern, Inter embedded, TrimBox and BleedBox set). Headless Chrome is for the large banners only, rendered in the background with a fresh profile, and it needs no glow anywhere on the page.

Proofs. Every print file ships with a FLAT PDF and a FLAT PNG beside it for checking on screen, and a SPOT PDF for the printer. The QR codes are error level H and keep a navy clear zone.

Print colour

The hex values in this guide are screen colours. A printer cannot make chartreuse #C8FF1A out of cyan, magenta, yellow, and black: it is a neon, "hot" colour, and on paper it comes out duller and more olive. These rules say what to ask for instead.

Screen#C8FF1A

The reference. What the site, the OG image, and every proof PNG show. Not a print value.

Spot ink, the real thingPANTONE 802 C

Fluorescent neon green. The only way the printed green matches the screen. Ask the shop for it by name, as a spot colour, and get a drawdown before the run.

Standard inkPANTONE 381 C

The nearest non-fluorescent Pantone. Use it when the shop has no neon ink. It is a strong lime, not neon, and it will read darker than the screen.

Process, the fallbackC 22 · M 0 · Y 94 · K 0

The best CMYK match for home, office, and digital-press printing. Expect it slightly duller and more olive than the screen. Never let it be the first choice for a card or a banner.

Professional print: spot colour. Cards, rack cards, and banners go to a shop as spot-colour artwork: PANTONE 2768 C for the navy and PANTONE 802 C for the green, with no CMYK conversion of either. The green knocks out of the navy, no overprint. If the shop says it cannot run a fluorescent ink, it prints 381 C and the proof is signed off knowing the green will be darker.

Home, office, and Canva: CMYK. Change the document colour mode to CMYK before you place any colour, then enter C 22, M 0, Y 94, K 0 for the green. Do not paste the hex and let the software convert it: the automatic conversion is worse than the values above. Check one printed sheet before printing the batch.

Off-white is the paper. Ink #F6F7F4 does not exist in print. The off-white text is a knockout to bright white stock, so the paper is the text colour. Choose the stock with that in mind: bright white, 16 pt for cards, uncoated or silk.

The navy. PANTONE 2768 C as a spot ink wherever the green is a spot ink. On a CMYK job the shop builds the navy from its own 2768 C conversion, and the proof decides. A navy that goes purple or grey is rejected.

Proof on paper, not on screen. Every print file ships with a FLAT PDF and PNG for checking layout on screen, and a SPOT PDF for the press. The screen files preview in brand RGB and will always look brighter than the print. The only proof that counts for colour is a printed one.

Tell the shop. Send the print spec text file with every order: the named inks, the note that 802 C is fluorescent and must not be converted, the stock, the bleed, and the QR clear zone. Ask which software and press they run before sending artwork, because a digital press cannot run a spot ink at all and needs the CMYK file.

Voice

VoiceClaw sounds like a straight-talking operator who has done the digging and is telling you what they found. Plain, specific, on the owner's side, and honest about limits.

Plain English

Words an owner-operator uses at the shop. No industry, startup, or software jargon. If a specialist term is unavoidable, say what it means the first time.

"Get the data out, including the old software nobody can export from."

From the owner's side

Write from where they sit: the truck, the desk, the phone that will not stop. Name the pain the way they say it, in their own quoted excuses.

"I'd have to check." "Let me get back to you." "It's in a text somewhere."

Concrete over clever

Real questions, real nouns, real numbers with names and dates. A question they asked someone this week beats any benefit statement.

"What did we quote them last time?"

We did the digging

Never imply the data was easy to get. The answers were buried and we clawed them out. Claim the work, not the magic. Say what is dug out: records.

"We dig out your records, we build one memory, we run it. You just talk to it."

Honest about limits

Say who it is not for. Say numbers belong on the call. Say the first customer is still an engagement in progress. Trust is the sale.

"We will tell you honestly whether your data can be clawed out, and what it would take."

Calm confidence

Short sentences. No exclamation marks. Fewer words on a wall read as confidence, not a gap. Let the answer do the talking.

"A simple question shouldn't take half your morning."

Punctuation and shape

No em dashes, ever. Periods, commas, and colons instead. Readers now read the long dash as a machine tell.

Short sentences. One idea each. Fragments are fine on a banner. "Then you just talk to it."

Numbers as words in prose ("five to fifty", "fourteen years"). Digits only inside a product answer, where the number is the point.

Questions in threes. The problem is shown as three real questions: jobs, paperwork, money. Keep that order when you reuse the pattern.

Quote the excuses. The three excuses in quotation marks stack from bright to muted over the photo. It is the only place the site uses quotation marks as a design element.

No exclamation marks, no superlatives. "Simple", "powerful", "seamless", "revolutionary" do not appear anywhere.
